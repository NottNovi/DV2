A data story on prices, pay and housing

Cost of Living in Australia

Australians are paying more for almost everything. Prices are 57% higher than in early 2010, and they rose another 4.3% in the past year alone. This page follows those rising costs from the national picture down to rent, house prices, energy and the household budget, and asks how far different pay packets stretch.

+57% Higher prices than in early 2010
+5.2% Rise in housing costs, the biggest share of spending
+8.7% Rise in transport costs, the fastest of any category

How the cost of living has changed

For Australia, since early 2010, consumer prices have climbed by about 57%. The steepest rise came in between 2022 and 2023. Melbourne city has followed the trends of the national figures closely throughout. The Consumer Price Index measures overall prices across the eight capital cities. It is not an exact measure of every household's costs, because each household spends differently.

Overall, the cost of living has increased significantly over the past decade. And regular increases may help the economy however they put pressure on household budgets for Australianas.

Where every $100 goes: low vs high income

This chart shows how low and high income households allocate every $100 of their budgets. Low income spends a larger share on essentials such as housing, food and utilities, leaving less for pleasure spending and little or nothing for savings. In contrast, high-income households allocate smaller shares to these necessities and more to savings and investments. This highlights unequal financial flexibility: rising living costs can place greater pressure on lower income households because they have less room in their budgets to absorb additional expenses. The chart compares budget proportions, not actual dollar spending, so a smaller share does not necessarily mean a household spends less overall.

Where spending meets rising prices

This chart shows how different spending categories relates to Australia’s inflation this year (2026) up until September 2026, with box sizes representing their share of the CPI basket and colours showing their annual price increases. Housing accounts for the largest share at 21.4%, with prices rising 5.2%, while transport recorded the fastest increase at 8.7%. Food and drinks also represent a substantial share of the basket, with prices increasing 3.2%. In contrast, clothing and household items experienced much smaller increases. Overall, the chart highlights that cost-of-living pressures depend on both how quickly prices rise and how much of the household budget those expenses represent.

How Earnings impact an individual's affordability

Living costs are only one part of affordability. Income also plays a crucial role in an individual's ability to afford these costs. This chart shows how earnings vary between occupations and pay levels, providing context for understanding who may find housing and other everyday expenses harder to afford.

From the data, it's clear that earnings can vary significantly across different occupations. It can be seen that many retail, hospitality, care and transport jobs stay below $90,000 even at senior level.

This would suggest that these workers may face greater challenges in affording basic necessities and that the increase in living costs will severely impact their purchasing power.

Where everyday costs are rising fastest

This heatmap shows how annual price increases change across Australia’s capital cities and spending categories. Hobart recorded the highest overall increase at 5.3%, while Melbourne had the lowest at 4.0%. Transport prices rose sharply in every city, reaching 10.6% in Adelaide and Darwin, while housing increases ranged from 3.8% in Melbourne to 7.6% in Darwin. Clothing and household items generally experienced smaller changes, including some price falls. Overall, the chart highlights that cost-of-living pressures differ depending on location and spending patterns. It shows where prices are rising fastest not necessarily which cities are the most expensive to live in.

How to cut your spending?

Keeping the lights on: electricity use across eastern Victoria

Energy is one of the bills households can least avoid. Across the AusNet network in eastern Victoria, homes in the cold high country use far more electricity in winter. Alpine households average about 29 kWh a day, roughly two-thirds above the area average. Switching to solar can significantly reduce electricity bills, as rooftop panels send excess power back to the grid in summer.

Conclusion

What this means for Australians

The cost of living in Australia has risen steadily for more than a decade. Consumer prices are about 57% higher than in early 2010, and they rose another 4.3% in the year to September 2026.

  • Housing is the biggest pressure. It is the largest share of what households spend, its prices rose 5.2% over the past year, and it added more to overall inflation than any other category.
  • Where you live matters. Price rises differ between capital cities, and rents for new leases in Victoria vary widely from council to council.
  • Pay does not stretch equally. Many retail, hospitality, care and transport jobs pay below $90,000 even at senior level, so rising essential costs take a bigger bite out of those budgets.
  • Australia is among the more expensive countries. Its everyday prices rank 18th of the 152 countries compared.

Rising costs are hard to avoid, but knowing where they are growing fastest, from housing and transport to energy use in colder regions, helps households plan where they can cut back.
